The Embarrassing Roth IRA Mistake I Made at 23 (And How to Fix It)

Putting money into a Roth IRA does not mean you actually invested it. Here is the rookie trap almost everyone falls into, and how to put your next $500 to work.

🧢 James Okafor · Money Realist · November 18, 2026 · 4 min read

You transfer $500 from your checking account into a shiny new Roth IRA, pat yourself on the back for being a responsible adult, and close the app.

I did this exact thing two years ago. I genuinely believed that simply putting the money into the account meant I was officially an investor. A month later, I logged in expecting compound interest to have performed some kind of financial miracle. Instead, my balance was exactly $500.00. Not a penny more.

It turns out nobody pulled me aside to explain the most basic rule of these accounts.

The empty shopping cart problem

Here is what I wish someone had told me: a Roth IRA is not an investment. It is just an account that holds your investments.

Think of it like an empty grocery cart. Opening the account and transferring your cash is simply walking into the supermarket and grabbing a cart. If you walk straight to the checkout counter without putting any groceries inside, you walk home with an empty stomach. When you transfer money into a Roth IRA, that cash just sits in a settlement fund until you actively buy something with it—like an index fund, an exchange-traded fund (ETF), or individual stocks.

A Roth IRA is not an investment; it is a tax-sheltered basket. If you do not choose what goes inside the basket, your cash just sits there gathering digital dust.

If you have opened one of these accounts recently, log in right now. Check if your money is actually invested or if it is just sitting in cash. You might be surprised.

Why this account belongs in your twenties

The rules around retirement accounts make them sound like they were designed to bore us to tears. But the underlying mechanics of a Roth IRA are actually built for people who are just starting out.

With a traditional retirement account, you get a tax break now, but you pay income taxes when you withdraw the money decades later. With a Roth IRA, you flip that script. You put in money you have already paid taxes on today. Because you paid your taxes upfront, every single dollar of growth can be withdrawn completely tax-free once you hit age 59 ½ (as long as the account has been open for at least five years).

When you are in your twenties and early in your career, your income—and therefore your tax rate—is probably lower than it will be down the road. Paying taxes now at a lower rate to get decades of tax-free growth later is a pretty sweet trade-off.

  • Contributions vs. Earnings: You can pull out your original contributions at any time without taxes or penalties. Life happens, and knowing you can touch your base deposits if things go south offers peace of mind.
  • The Annual Limit: For 2026 and 2027, the IRS caps your annual direct contributions at $7,500 (or $8,600 if you are 50 or older).
  • Income Ceilings: You can contribute the full amount if your modified adjusted gross income is under $153,000 for 2026 (or under $158,000 for 2027) as a single filer.

That contribution rule is huge for peace of mind. While you should try your hardest to leave retirement money alone, knowing your initial contributions are not permanently locked in a vault makes putting that first $500 away a lot less terrifying.

What to do with your first $500 this week

You do not need $7,500 sitting in your checking account to care about this. You do not even need $1,000.

If you have an extra $100 or $500 sitting in an everyday savings account, consider moving a slice of it into a Roth IRA. But do not stop at the deposit screen. Once the cash clears, pick an investment. For most of us who do not want to spend our evenings analyzing balance sheets, broad-market index funds or ETFs that track hundreds of companies are a solid, low-cost place to start.

Nobody knows where the stock market is heading next month or next year.

Markets bounce around constantly, and seeing your balance dip right after you invest can make your stomach churn. But building wealth in your twenties is not about timing the market perfectly. It is about getting your dollars into the game early so time can do the heavy lifting for you.
