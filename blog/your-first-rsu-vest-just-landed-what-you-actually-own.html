📈 Equity & RSUs

Your First RSU Vest Just Landed: What You Actually Own

Seeing company stock show up in your account feels like free money until taxes hit. Here is how equity comp works and what to do with it.

🧢 James Okafor · Money Realist · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

You open your email on a random Tuesday morning and spot a notification from a brokerage company you barely remember signing up for during new-hire orientation. Your quarterly equity grant has vested. When I first saw news this week that an executive named Spinks at SBH received 25,987 Restricted Stock Units according to a Form 4 filing, my immediate thought wasn't about executive compensation. My thought was about how mystifying those three little letters—RSU—felt the first time someone promised them to me in an offer letter.

TL;DR: RSUs are treated by the IRS as straight cash income the moment they vest · Automatic share withholding usually covers less tax than you actually owe · Treating company shares as a bonus to diversify protects you from losing your savings and your paycheck at the same time

What happens when RSUs actually land in your account

When you sign an offer with equity, recruiters talk about your total compensation as one big, shiny number. They tell you your salary is $110,000 and your equity package is $60,000 over four years. It sounds like you are getting a vault of stock handed to you on day one. In reality, you hold a promise written on paper and nothing else.

That promise is what the word 'restricted' means. You cannot touch those shares, borrow against them, or sell them until you hit specific calendar checkpoints known as vesting dates. Most companies use a one-year cliff followed by quarterly vests. You work for twelve months to earn your first chunk, and after that, small slices unlock every three months.

When that vesting date arrives, the company does not cut you a check. They deposit real shares of stock into an account at a custodian brokerage. The second those shares clear, they belong to you completely. But there is a catch that caught me completely off guard the first time I saw it: half your shares seem to vanish instantly.

That disappearance is called 'sell to cover.' Because the IRS treats vested shares as ordinary income—just like your regular Friday paycheck—taxes are due on the spot. Your company automatically sells a slice of your newly arrived shares to pay estimated federal, state, and payroll taxes before you even see the balance.

The worked math: A $60,000 grant in real life

Let's run through the real math so you know what to expect when a vest hits your account. Suppose you earn a base salary of $110,000 per year. When you were hired, you received an RSU grant valued at $60,000, vesting evenly across four years with quarterly vesting.

That means each year, you are scheduled to vest $15,000 worth of stock. Spread across four quarters, each vesting event is worth roughly $3,750 before taxes. Let's say the company stock is trading at $50 per share on the exact day your quarter vests. That means you are owed 75 shares.

Now the tax math kicks in. The federal government mandates a statutory withholding rate of 22% on supplemental income up to one million dollars. Add 6.2% for Social Security, 1.45% for Medicare, and an estimated 5% for your state income tax. Your total automatic withholding comes out to roughly 34.65%.

To cover that tax bill on your 75 shares ($1,299.38 in taxes), your brokerage automatically sells 26 shares on the open market ($1,300 worth). You end up with 49 shares deposited into your account, worth $2,450. You did not lose that money; it went straight to the government to cover your income tax.

The common mistake: Treating company stock like a loyalty test

The single biggest mistake I see colleagues make is treating their vested shares like a souvenir or a sign of personal devotion to their employer. They watch those 49 shares land in their account, and they do nothing. Quarter after quarter, the pile grows.

Holding onto every share means your financial life is doubled down on a single business. Your bi-weekly paycheck already depends on the health and management of your employer. If the company hits a rough patch, two bad things can happen at the exact same moment: your portfolio plunges, and your department faces layoffs.

Between January 3, 2022 and October 12, 2022, the S&P 500 fell about 25%. Many individual tech and growth companies fell by 50% to 70% during that same window. People who left their vests untouched watched years of hard-earned compensation evaporate right when tech hiring freezes started.

Keeping all your net worth in your employer's stock is not loyalty. It is an unhedged bet on a single horse.

The 'Same as Cash' rule of thumb

If you feel guilty or anxious about selling your shares, use this mental test every single time a vest notification pings your phone. Pretend that instead of stock, your employer handed you an envelope containing $2,450 in crisp cash as a quarterly performance bonus.

Ask yourself honestly: would you take that cash, open your brokerage app, and purchase $2,450 worth of your company's stock? Would you do that instead of paying down your high-interest credit card, adding to your emergency fund, or buying a broad index fund that owns hundreds of companies?

If your answer is no, then keeping the stock makes zero sense. Keeping the stock is mathematically identical to buying it with cash today. Your cost basis resets on the vest date to the fair market value, so there is almost no capital gains tax if you sell immediately.

You are simply taking your pay in cash and allocating it where your personal life actually needs it.

What to do this week

If you have RSUs vesting soon—or a pile of vested shares sitting in an account you have avoided opening—here are the steps to take right now:

  • Check your supplemental tax rate: Log into your equity portal and review your tax settings. If your household tax bracket is higher than the standard 22% federal supplemental rate, set aside cash for tax season so you do not get surprised by an underpayment penalty in April.
  • Set an automatic sell preference: Most equity brokerages allow you to choose an automatic 'sell all shares on vest' option. Turning this on removes the emotion and prevents you from trying to time the daily fluctuations of your company's stock price.
  • Direct the proceeds to clear priorities: Route that cash into a high-yield savings account if you lack a 3-month emergency fund, pay down debt above 7% interest, or sweep it into a low-cost total market index fund.

Do I have to pay extra capital gains tax if I sell my RSUs the day they vest?

No, because the price at which the shares vest becomes your tax basis. If your shares vest at $50 each and you sell them that afternoon for $50.05, you only owe capital gains tax on the five-cent gain per share.

What happens to my unvested shares if I quit or get laid off?

Any shares that have not reached their official vesting date are canceled and returned to the company's equity pool. You keep only the shares that fully vested before your termination date.

Is my manager or executive team notified when I sell my vested shares?

Unless you are an executive officer filing SEC Form 4 documents like the ones Spinks filed at SBH, nobody in your management chain gets an alert when you sell your vested stock. Your equity compensation is your paycheck, and what you do with your pay is entirely your private business.
